Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level caused by the gravitational pull of the Moon and the Sun on the oceans, combined with the rotation of the Earth. Most coastlines see two high tides and two low tides in a little under twenty-five hours, because the Moon returns to the same position overhead about fifty minutes later each day. Some coasts see only one high and one low tide a day, and others see a mixed pattern in which the two high tides differ markedly in height.

The height difference between high and low water is called the tidal range. It is largest shortly after the new and full Moon, when the pull of the Sun and the Moon act along the same line; these are spring tides. It is smallest near the first and last quarter, when the two pulls act at right angles; these are neap tides. The cycle from one spring tide to the next takes about two weeks.

Local geography changes the tide far more than astronomy alone would suggest. In the open ocean the range is usually well under a metre. A funnel-shaped bay or a shallow shelf can amplify it to ten metres or more, while an enclosed sea with a narrow entrance may show almost no tide at all. The time of high water also shifts along a coast, since the tide moves as a long wave around ocean basins.

Tide tables list the predicted times and heights of high and low water for a given port. They are computed from long records of observed water levels, which are broken down into many regular components with different periods. Adding the components back together for a future date gives the prediction. Weather is not included: strong onshore wind and low air pressure raise the water above the predicted level, and offshore wind and high pressure lower it.

Nautical charts show depths relative to a low-water reference level called chart datum, chosen so that the water is rarely shallower than the charted figure. To find the actual depth at a given moment, the height of tide from the table is added to the charted depth. Heights of bridges and overhead cables are given above a high-water level for the same reason, so that the clearance shown is rarely overstated.

Tidal streams are the horizontal movements of water that accompany the rise and fall. In a narrow channel the stream may run at several knots and reverse direction roughly every six hours, with a short period of slack water in between. Slack water does not always coincide with high or low water; in long estuaries the stream can keep running upstream for an hour or more after the level has begun to fall.

A paper chart is drawn on a projection, a rule for transferring the curved surface of the Earth onto a flat sheet. The Mercator projection, used for most navigational charts, keeps compass bearings as straight lines, at the cost of stretching areas more and more towards the poles. The scale of a chart is the ratio between a distance on the paper and the same distance on the ground; a large-scale chart covers a small area in detail, and a small-scale chart covers a large area with little detail.

Latitude is measured in degrees north or south of the equator, and longitude in degrees east or west of the prime meridian. One minute of latitude corresponds to one nautical mile, which is why distances on a chart are read from the latitude scale at the side and never from the longitude scale at the top or bottom. Contour lines on land and depth contours at sea join points of equal height or depth; where they lie close together the slope is steep.

Charts are revised as surveys improve and as sandbanks, channels and buoys move. Each chart carries its edition date and a record of corrections applied since printing. A chart based on an old survey may show soundings taken with a lead line at wide spacing, so an unmarked area is not necessarily free of hazards.
